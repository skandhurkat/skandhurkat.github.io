A Brief History of LLMs

Skand Hurkat · June 18, 2026 · 66 min read

Large language models (LLMs) are all the rage these days. It’s not uncommon to hear someone say, ‘I asked ChatGPT’, or indeed, to have a sitting US Senator publish an ‘interview’ they had with a large language model to their YouTube channel. In this post, like Toto, I pull back the curtain on LLMs, and I trace their origins going all the way back to the very first computer that learnt from data. I highlight the discoveries that – in my opinion – were key in leading up to this moment. I will also show how to build our very own chatbot around a language model, how to give it access to system tools, and demystify the ‘Agentic AI’ buzzword.

AI is a buzzword

I hate the term artificial intelligence, or AI for short. Artificial intelligence as a term dates back to a 1956 workshop at Dartmouth College organized by John McCarthy. He thought the term ‘automata theory’ wasn’t catchy enough, and therefore, in a stroke of genius, named the workshop, ‘The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence.’ The term has stuck with us ever since, because intelligence is highly prized among humans, and the quest to build an artificial human, or to discover what makes us humans, has captured our imagination for a long time.

In their book ‘AI Snake Oil’, Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor describe AI as the term used for any technology that is just beyond the cusp of what feels possible in the dominant computing paradigm of the time. They make the case that there was a time when spell check was considered an AI technology, and today, it’s just expected in every word processor. Autocomplete also its moment of glory as an AI technology, yet, today, we go on pecking a few characters on our mobile phone keyboards, and then just select the next few words that pop up. As an aside, by the end of this blog post, you’ll see that these LLMs of the day have evolved from autocomplete on steroids, which makes it perhaps fitting that autocomplete was at one time considered to be AI.

To borrow an analogy from Karen Hao, the term AI is a catch-all for a broad category, like ’transportation’. When talking about transportation, it’s useful to know whether we’re talking bikes or rockets. I, therefore, prefer to refer to each technology by its proper name instead of a catch-all buzzword. It is my hope that you’ll develop an appreciation for the technology behind these LLMs, but that you’ll also find these de-mystified. LLMs are promising to be wonderful tools if used properly, and understanding how they work goes a long way in figuring out the tasks at which they will be useful. LLMs raise philosophical questions about the nature of our language and about what qualifies as intelligence. By the end of this post, I hope we can all agree that the imitation game – aka the Turing Test – was a mistake, and understand why LLMs are so effective at convincing us of things we already wish to believe.

The Perceptron

We start our journey back in second half of the 1950s, at my alma mater – Cornell University. Frank Rosenblatt was obsessed with how brains work, and in trying to explain the behaviour of neurons, created a machine the likes of which hadn’t been seen before. This machine, called the Mark I Perceptron comprised an array of photocells that could see an image on a card, project it on a retina, and then classify it as one of a number of categories. As Rosenblatt wrote in his 1958 paper (non-paywalled) published in Psychological Review, the key idea behind the perceptron was to provide a precise, analytical model of a brain. (The paper is a class act at throwing shade at other scientists who ‘have generally been less exact in their formulations and far from rigorous in their analysis’, so it’s worth a read.)

Rosenblatt’s perceptron had a number of photo cells (called S or sensory units) arranged in a grid. These S units were randomly connected to a set of associative or A units that mimicked Rosenblatt’s idea of random neural connections in the retina. An S unit would output a voltage V if the light reaching that unit exceeded a threshold, a 0 otherwise. S units could act in either excitatory or inhibitory manner at the A unit, controlled via a plugboard. An excitatory connection between an S unit and an A unit would result in a +V at the A unit, and an inhibitory connection would result in a -V when the sensory unit was active. If the sum at an A unit exceeded a threshold, the A unit would output a voltage, or a 0 if the threshold wasn’t met. The voltage output of an A unit, however, wasn’t fixed, but could be adjusted through a potentiometer that was controlled using an electric motor, and could vary from -V’ to V’. These A units were connected to a set of response (R) units. If the total sum of voltage from A units to an R unit exceeded the R unit threshold, the R unit would output a voltage, or output 0 otherwise. However, the R unit had some additional feedback mechanisms that made learning possible. The first mechanism was an inhibit circuit. Whenever an R unit activated (i.e., output a voltage, or as we’d call it today, a 1), the inhibition circuit zeroed out the inputs from all the A units that did not feed into this R unit, ensuring that no other R unit could activate. Further, the inhibition circuit also latched up the R unit that was active, ensuring that it would remain active. A zeroing unit supplied voltage to motors driving the potentiometers controlling A unit outputs (at a very low speed) to drive the sum of input voltages to this R unit to around 0 V.

Rosenblatt’s design of the Perceptron was inspired by his understanding of biological neurons. A neuron receives electrical impulses from its neighbouring neurons, and if these impulses exceed a threshold within a period of time, the neuron itself fires an impulse to its downstream neighbours. Over time, a neuron adapts itself to the impulse pattern of its neighbours, reinforcing itself resulting in learning. A neuron doesn’t necessarily have the mechanism to inhibit the inputs of other neurons – that bit was added by Rosenblatt to make his machine produce mutually exclusive outputs (that is, have only one R unit output 1 at any time, and have all others output a 0).

The training mechanism for the Perceptron is described in its operator’s manual. All A units shared a common threshold voltage set using a potentiometer, and the threshold was generally selected to be one less than the number of inhibitory connections subtracted from the number of excitatory connections (an appendix lists some valid connections from the S to the A units). R units had individual thresholds that were carefully calibrated to a reference threshold voltage. The machine had multiple learning modes, from an unsupervised learning (called spontaneous learning in the manual) mode that would pick up on patterns and classify them to one of the eight R unit connections, to a supervised learning (called forced learning in the manual) mode where an operator would indicate the desired R unit outputs for any given input.

Let’s work through what would happen if the Perceptron was provided with a stimulus, in its case, a projector shining a light through a slide directly at the photo cell array making up the S units. The S units would fire depending on whether they were in light or shadow, causing their connected A units to fire depending on their inputs. At this point, one of the R units would have the strongest signal from the A units, depending on the values encoded within the potentiometers of these A units. This R unit would immediately latch up and inhibit all other R units, and then, crucially, provide a feedback signal to the motors to drive its own input voltage towards zero (the Perceptron used positive and negative voltages). This would reinforce the connections which caused this R unit to fire, ensuring that a similar set of inputs would then also result in the R unit firing in the future. So, if the operator showed a distinct set of images that caused different R units to fire, over time, the Perceptron would enhance the differences in the signals through its feedback mechanism, causing other inputs to fire R units that were more closely associated with the categories already learnt. This was spontaneous learning, or as we’d call it today, unsupervised learning. The machine, when exposed to different stimuli, would spontaneously, without any human supervision, learn the differences between these inputs. The contrast was forced learning, or what we’d call supervised learning today. In this mode, the operator explicitly set as active the desired R unit for a given stimulus, and this exercised the same reinforcement mechanisms that made spontaneous learning possible.

The Perceptron was a machine that challenged conventional wisdom of the time, and LLMs today challenge our collective contemporary understanding of what a computer can and cannot do. If you had told me five years ago that I would spend my time arguing with a random word generator which would more often than not produce functional code, I would have laughed at you. LLMs today can indeed provide the illusion of thinking, of reading and writing, and they are built upon the same principles as the original Mark I Perceptron.

Today (or at least back in 2012 when I took a machine learning class before deep neural networks and LLMs replaced the machine learning course), the perceptron was a little side note discussed in about fifteen minutes in the classroom. Today, the perceptron is taught as a linear classifier – the perceptron learns a hyperplane that separates the inputs into one of two in an N-dimensional space. Given an input vector \( \overrightarrow{x} = \left[x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1} \right] \), the perceptron learns weights \( \overrightarrow{w} = \left[ w_0, w_1, \ldots, w_{n-1} \right] \), such that the dot product of the weight vector with the input vector compared with a threshold determines the classification of the input into one of two categories. That is, the output of the perceptron \( y \) is defined as:

$$ y = \begin{cases} 1 & \text {if } \overrightarrow{w} \cdot \overrightarrow{x} - b > 0 \\ 0 & \text {otherwise} \end{cases} $$

The perceptron’s learning algorithm gets a similarly concise explanation in a modern course. We start with a random weight vector and threshold, and feed input samples through the perceptron. If the perceptron correctly classifies the input, great. If not, we nudge the weights and threshold slightly in the direction of the inputs, depending on how far off the perceptron was in its classification. If our weights and bias at time step \(t\) were \(\overrightarrow{w_t}\) and \(b_t\) respectively, given an input \(\overrightarrow{x_t}\) and its expected classification \(d_t\), we change the weights such that

$$ \begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{w_{t+1}} &= \overrightarrow{w_t} + \eta (d_t - y_t) \overrightarrow{x_t} \\ b_{t+1} &= b_t + \eta (d_t - y_t) \end{aligned} $$

where \( \eta \) is a learning rate that can be tuned to determine the quality of the training. Large values will result in the weights changing rapidly, which would result in faster training, but may result in over-correction when the algorithm gets results wrong, and smaller values will result in slower changes. This is very similar to the training algorithm used by the Mark I Perceptron with its zeroing unit changing the output voltages of active A units.

Multi-layer perceptrons

The Perceptron – as described by Rosenblatt or in a modern classroom – has a fatal flaw. Because it’s a linear classifier, it cannot learn functions that are non-linear. Consider learning a function like \(\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2 > 0.5\) – a perceptron will never be able to learn this function given all the training time in the world. This was one of Marvin Minsky’s criticisms of the perceptron, and it probably led to a decline in interest in artificial neural networks in the late 1960s and 70s.

In 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams published a technique (non-paywalled) of using multiple neuron-like units connected over multiple layers, and trained using a novel algorithm they termed back-propagation. (Technically, Yann LeCun published his paper a year earlier in 1985, but that paper is in French, and has escaped attention from the English-speaking community, with only about a thousand citations compared to about fifty-thousand for Rumelhart et al.) This method iteratively minimised the difference between the output of the network and the desired output, and the hidden layers of the network allowed it to learn important features of the task domain. The hidden layers allowed the neural network to learn more complex representations than could be learnt with a single perceptron, but make training more complicated – how do you apportion the amount any one layer or neuron plays in the final error?

The back-propagation technique described in this paper very elegantly solves the problem of apportioning error to different layers using the chain rule of differentiation familiar to any high-school calculus student. Let’s treat each layer of the neural network as a distinct function of the weights of that level and the output of the previous level (or the input in case of the first level). In other words

$$ y = f_{n}(w_{n}, f_{n-1}(w_{n-1}, \ldots)) $$

We can define a loss function that is a measure of how far away the output produced from this network is from the desired output \(d\).

$$ L = \frac{1}{2}(y - d)^2 $$

And the goal here is to change the weights \(w_i\) such that we minimise the loss. The general idea with minimisation problems is that we compute gradients, or partial derivatives and take a step in the direction of negative gradients. This is similar to walking down a hill to the valley. If you keep sampling the local area and taking a step down the steepest gradient or slope, you will eventually reach a place where the gradient is flat, and that would be the local minimum. This method is not guaranteed to get you to a global minimum (for example, you may find yourself in a local valley, but the terrain falls even further down the next hill), and that is a shortcoming of this approach. This method of descending down the steepest gradient is called, intuitively, gradient descent.

(Note, the next few paragraphs dive into some calculus. If you would prefer to skip the maths, you can jump to about here.)

Gradient descent boils down to this simple algorithm – we move each of the weights \(w_i\) by a value proportional to the partial derivative of the loss function with respect to \(w_i\). In other words, take a step down the steepest slope of our hypothetical landscape. Mathematically: $$ w_{i, t+1} = w_{i, t} + \eta \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{i}} $$ \(\eta\), once again, is a learning rate, something that determines how big a step we take at each iteration. Too large, and we may overshoot our desired valley. Too small, and we’ll take a long time to get to the valley.

The important quantity that we must calculate, therefore, is \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}\). This is how much we should nudge each weight in the model to reduce the loss. This can be calculated through the use of the chain-rule for differentiation. Remember that the chain rule states that: $$ \frac{\partial f\left(g\left(x\right)\right)}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f\left(g\left(x\right)\right)}{\partial g(x)} \times \frac{\partial g(x)}{\partial x} $$

We can apply the same rule for computing the partial derivatives of the loss function with respect to the weights: $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_i} &= \frac{\partial \frac{1}{2} (y - d)^2}{\partial w_i} \\ &= \frac{\partial \frac{1}{2} (y - d)^2}{\partial y} \times \frac{\partial y}{\partial w_i} \\ &= (y - d) \times \frac{\partial y}{\partial w_i} \\ &= (y - d) \times \frac{\partial f_{n}\left(w_{n}, f_{n-1}\left(w_{n-1}, \ldots\right)\right)}{\partial w_i} \\ &= (y - d) \times \frac{\partial f_{n}\left(w_{n}, f_{n-1}\left(w_{n-1}, \ldots\right)\right)}{\partial f_{n-1}\left(w_{n-1}, \ldots\right)} \times \ldots \times \frac{f_{i}\left(w_{i}, f_{i-1}\left(w_{i} \ldots\right)\right)}{\partial w_{i}} \end{aligned} $$

This maths shows us that we only need two calculations at each step:

  1. The partial derivative of each function with respect to the previous function that feeds into it.
  2. The partial derivative of each function with respect to the weights within that function.

That’s easy to perform mechanically, and this means we can stack as many functions as we want, and use the same algorithm for training a neural network of any depth. This remains one of the most important discoveries in neural networks, and the technique – called back-propagation because we back-propagate error gradients through the network – is what is used even today to train LLMs.

Back-propagation requires us to calculate partial derivatives for each function, which also implies that each function must be differentiable. The authors of this paper changed the hard functions used in the perceptron to soft, differentiable versions. For example, the hard threshold function used in the Perceptron: $$ H(x) = \begin{cases} 1 &\text{ if } x > 0 \\ 0 &\text{ otherwise} \end{cases} $$ is replaced with a soft version (a sigmoid function) that gradually shifts from 0 to 1 as the value of the input changes from negative to positive. The soft version has a non-zero gradient at every point, whereas the hard version has a zero gradient at almost every point except at 0. A ball placed on the hard threshold function would have nowhere to roll down, the soft versions are necessary for learning via gradient descent. $$ \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} $$

Similarly, the hard maximum used in the Perceptron (the R unit with the highest voltage suppresses all other R units) is replaced with a soft maximum: $$ \text{softmax}(\overrightarrow{x})_i = \frac{e^{x_i}}{ \sum_j e^{x_j}} $$

Now, let’s walk through an example of how we may use back-propagation for a simple two-layer network. Consider a network that takes in two inputs, produces two intermediate (hidden) values, and then uses the two intermediate values to produce a final prediction.

Our network may be described through the following two steps:

$$ \begin{aligned} \left[ \begin{matrix} i_1 \\ i_2 \end{matrix} \right] &= \sigma \left( \left[ \begin{matrix} w_{11} & w_{12} \\ w_{21} & w_{22} \end{matrix} \right] \times \left[ \begin{matrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{matrix} \right]+ \left[ \begin{matrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{matrix} \right]\right) \\ y &= \sigma \left( \left[ \begin{matrix} v_1 & v_2 \end{matrix} \right] \times \left[ \begin{matrix} i_1 \\ i_2 \end{matrix} \right] + c \right) \end{aligned} $$

Or, to put it differently, without the matrix maths:

$$ \begin{aligned} \hat{i_1} &= w_{11} x_1 + w_{12} x_2 + b_1 \\ i_1 &= \sigma \left( \hat{i_1} \right) \\ \hat{i_2} &= w_{21} x_1 + w_{22} x_2 + b_2 \\ i_1 &= \sigma \left( \hat{i_2} \right) \\ \hat{y} &= v_1 i_1 + v_2 i_2 + c \\ y &= \sigma \left( \hat{y} \right) \end{aligned} $$

We need the partial derivatives of the loss with respect to each of the weights. But, as we’ve shown previously, we can use the chain rule which limits the partial derivatives we must compute. For the second layer, we only need the partial derivatives of \(y\) with respect to \(v_1, v_2, c\).

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial y}{\partial v_1} &= \frac{\partial \sigma (\hat{y})}{\partial \hat{y}} \times \frac{\hat{y}}{v_1} \\ &= \sigma(\hat{y})(1 - \sigma(\hat{y})) \times i_1 \end{aligned} $$ and similarly for the derivatives with respect to \(v_2\) and \(c\).

Next, we compute the derivatives with respect to the weights in the first layer thus: $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial y}{\partial w_{11}} &= \frac{\partial y}{\partial i_1} \times \frac{\partial i_1}{\partial w_{11}} \\\\ &= \frac{\partial y}{\partial \hat{y}} \times \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial i_1} \times \frac{\partial i_1}{\partial \hat{i_1}} \times \frac{\partial \hat{i_1}}{\partial w_{11}} \\\\ &= \underbrace{\sigma(\hat{y})(1 - \sigma(\hat{y})) \times v_1}_\text{from output layer} \times \underbrace{\sigma(\hat{i_1}) (1-\sigma(\hat{ i_1 })) \times x_1}_\text{from hidden layer} \end{aligned} $$ and similarly for derivatives with respect to other weights.

These demos should make it clear that learning in a neural network is simply a matter of turning the mathematical crank and nudging the weights until the network produces the desired output. There is no intelligence inherent to matrix multiplications. However, the anthropomorphised term has stayed with us since the days of the Perceptron.

If we try the demo above with a few random starting values, we notice that the functions the multi-layer perceptron (MLP, or, neural network, to use the buzzword from the era) learns in order to separate the two sets of points are not necessarily straight lines, but some complex curves. Given that the data were generated with a simple mathematical formula \( (x_1 - x_2)^2 > 0.5 \), the true discriminating function is a pair of parallel lines. The MLP, however, learns an entirely different function that just happens to work for the points in the training data set, which you will observe by clicking on the “Zoom Out” button to view a larger area. This phenomenon is called over-fitting, and is a major drawback of MLPs. The chances of an MLP over-fitting may be reduced by providing additional training data, but the danger is never completely eliminated. When I was an undergrad, I was chastised for using a neural network in my project to identify car make and model, because, ‘Everyone knows that neural networks are sloppy machine learning techniques that give you no visibility into why they work.’ This was two years before Krizhevsky et al published their paper that caused deep neural networks to once again burst into prominence and kicked off the current AI hype cycle ¯\_(ツ)_/¯.

Now that we understand the mechanics of back-propagation and gradient descent, it’s important to understand that most neural networks are simply a matter of stacking more and more of these layers and letting computers iteratively calculate gradients and update weights until the algorithm converges. Typically, machine learning researchers use a separate test dataset that is never used for training the neural network, and then adjust model hyperparameters, like the number of layers or the size of hidden layers to produce a model that performs the best on the task.

Processing text with neural networks

With the basics of a neural network in place, let’s understand how these networks can be used to process text. Remember that a neural network is just a bunch of matrix operations, so we need a way to transform text into a multi-dimensional vector that can be fed into a neural network.

It is helpful, when converting text into vectors for consumption by a neural network, that the encoded vectors represent some sort of semantic distance between the concepts articulated by words. For example, we would want the distance between the words ‘king’ and ‘queen’ to be the same as the distance between ‘man’ and ‘woman’, because the only difference between both pairs of words is moving from a masculine to a feminine gender. Word2Vec is a popular technique that does just this. While I won’t cover Word2Vec in this blog post, it’s fun to look at a visualisation of the generated embeddings.

Language imparts meaning not just through the words in a sentence, but also the order in which these words appear. ‘I did play video games but I did not write this blog post’ and ‘I did not play video games but I did write this blog post’ have the same words, but opposite meanings just by changing the position of one word. A neural network must, therefore, be able to understand the relative positions of words along with the words themselves. It must also be able to determine when it needs to remember or forget previously seen words – words within a phrase and clause may lose their relative importance when looking at the next phrase or clause.

One technique for imparting memory into a computer system is to feed the output of the system back into itself. This technique was adapted by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in 1997 (non-paywalled) to create the long short-term memory (LSTM) neural network. In 2014, Cho et al. simplified LSTM networks to gated recurrent units (GRU). The basic idea in these networks is similar – the network contains some hidden state that can be fed back into the network as input along with the next word that is processed by the network. Two or three small gates (essentially matrix multiplications, like a simple perceptron or a small neural network) determine how much the hidden state is affected by the new word or the old hidden state.

Encoder-decoder networks

Cho et al proposed not just the gated recurrent unit, but also a new encoder-decoder mechanism for translating text between languages. This mechanism consisted of two sets of recurrent units. First, an encoder recurrent unit would accept an arbitrary length phrase and summarize it into a single vector. Second, a decoder recurrent unit would take this summary from the encoder and output the translated phrase.

Also in 2014, Sutskever et al used the same encoder-decoder mechanism proposed by Cho et al, but made a few changes. First, they preferred the LSTM mechanism for memory over the GRU mechanism. Second, they made their LSTM four layers deep because deeper networks (i.e., with more hidden layers) perform better than shallower networks. Third, they trained the network by feeding in the input sentence reversed and the output sentence in the right order. That is, if ABC translated as WXYZ, the network was trained to accept CBA and output WXYZ. This method was preferred because it performed better; the authors justified it by asserting that it allowed the network to make short-term connections more effectively, as the word C would be more likely to be translated as W, allowing the distance to be closer.

The assertion made by Sutskever et al that reversing the input sentence would result in a better translation doesn’t really make sense (Ilya Sutskever co-founded OpenAI, which makes my critique of his research sound funny) – why would one prioritize shorter connections from the end of the input sentence to the beginning of the output sentence, but suffer longer connections from the beginning of the input sentence to the end of the output sentence? In fact, the authors admit as much in that paper; stating that the strong performance of this network was contrary to their expectations. What if we instead encoded the input sentence in both directions before handing the encoded form over to the decoder? In 2013, Graves et al (non-paywalled) used this architecture – which they called a bidirectional LSTM – for speech recognition.

Attention mechanisms

Encoder-decoder networks suffer from a fundamental problem. The network architecture encodes all the information from the input into a single vector. This means that as the length of the input phrase or sentence increases the information from each word in that sentence gets diluted. Cho et al demonstrated translation between phrases about four to five words in length with a simple GRU. Sutskever et al, with their deeper LSTM network could translate sentences around forty or fifty words in length.

Humans don’t usually translate by summarizing entire paragraphs. We typically look at relevant words or phrases, translating a few words at a time. Some pairs of languages have different grammatical construction, a human translating between these languages would typically choose the order in which they read the source material. Bahdanau et al proposed a mechanism where a neural network could learn to align text while translating. This method still builds on the encoder-decoder recurrent architecture that was proposed by Cho et al and Sutskever et al, but crucially adds an alignment model between the encoder and decoder layers that amplifies the relevant positions in the encoder input while decoding the translation. They also use a bidirectional RNN instead of the unidirectional RNNs used by Cho et al or Sutskever et al. The alignment network itself is a simple neural network that is trained on input-output pairs. This is a trick that is often repeated in neural networks – just like GRUs and LSTMs use simple matrix multiplications, essentially the equivalent of a perceptron to remember and forget previous context, we can start introducing smaller neural networks within larger, more complicated neural networks.

Bahdanau et al’s paper also had pictures showing exactly how this proposed alignment mechanism allowed the network to focus on only the relevant words from the input when translating a sentence between English and French. Machine learning researchers love this sort of visualisation, as it allows us to have some insights into whether models are performing as we expect – otherwise, models are often black boxes.

Sukhbaatar et al in 2015 proposed an interesting mechanism for storing and looking up items in a neural network’s memory. Their mechanism processed the input items to be stored in memory using a neural network. In the simplest case, this could be a simple feed-forward neural network, but it could also be a more complex network. The inputs \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\) are thus converted into memory representations \(m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_n\). Then, an incoming query \(q\) is converted once again into a memory representation \(u\) using a (potentially different) neural network. Similarity between the query and the inputs stored in memory is then calculated using a dot product of the memory representations. That is to say, the similarity between \(q\) and \(x_i\) is related to \(u^T m_i\). The authors use a softmax function to represent the similarity as a probability distribution – the output vector from a softmax function always sums to one by construction.

Chen et al proposed a modification to this design for a reading comprehension task. A reading comprehension task needs a network to be able to point to the part of text containing the answer, this is perfect for an end-to-end memory network, where the inputs are the words in the paragraph to comprehend, and the query is related to the question asked. Unlike Sukhbaatar et al’s use of the dot product for computing similarity between the query and memory, Chen et al used a bilinear attention mechanism where they added an additional weight matrix – \(\text{softmax}(q^T W_s m_i)\).

Attention is all you need

The attention mechanisms discussed so far build on top of recurrent neural networks. Even though we are directly computing similarity between input and output words for translation or between the provided text and the query for comprehension, we are still relying on a recurrent neural network for processing the underlying text – either in a uni- or bi-directional manner. In 2017, Vaswani et al published a paper titled ‘Attention is all you need’, an apt name considering that the main idea proposed in this paper was that we could get rid of the recurrent network altogether – all we needed was the attention mechanism. This architecture, called the Transformer, is the basis of all modern large language models.

Vaswani et al were still concerned with the problem of translating text from one language to another. This was also about the same time that Google was working on their tensor processing unit (TPU), built specifically to multiply large matrices. Recurrent networks, on the other hand, use long chains of dependent matrix-vector multiplications, which cannot be efficiently processed on a TPU-like computer. This, in my opinion, motivated the authors to propose a neural network that was almost entirely based around matrix multiplications. The authors cite four motivating factors for this network architecture:

  1. The computational complexity per layer is low.
  2. The computation can be parallelised.
  3. Attention is an easier way to capture long-range dependencies compared to LSTM networks.
  4. Attention (maybe) is more interpretable.

The transformer, as proposed by Vaswani et al, borrowed heavily from the contemporary design of LSTM-like networks for translation. It had a stack of encoders feeding into a stack of decoders, just like the ones proposed by Sutskever et al or Cho et al. The input was fed in to the encoder stack, the output of the encoder stack along with previously decoded outputs fed into the decoder stack. The difference was in the computation within each layer of the stack. Unlike an RNN or LSTM, Vaswani et al proposed a multi-headed attention mechanism.

The attention mechanism proposed by Vaswani et al is extremely simple. If we encode each input word into a vector, we get a sequence of vectors \(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\). We can concatenate these vectors row-wise to get an input matrix \(X\). If we embed each word into an \(e\) dimensional space and we have \(l\) words, our matrix \(X\) has \(l\) rows and \(e\) columns. We want an all-to-all attention between input words – \(XX^T\) gets us that. We also want to move this back into an \(e\) dimensional space, so we can multiply this by another \(X\) matrix – \( (XX^T) X \) – to go back to an \(l \times e\) matrix.

Simply multiplying the input matrices thus will probably not yield useful results. After all, we’re talking neural networks, and if there’s one thing you should take away from this blog, it is that we can always add a matrix multiplication and a non-linear operation to a neural network to produce a better result. Vaswani et al do exactly this – thrice. Let’s add three matrices \(W_q, W_k, W_v\), and let’s multiply each of the terms with these matrices. Our attention mechanism now looks like \(\left((XW_q)(XW_k)^T\right) (XW_v)\), or \((QK^T)V\). (Previously in this blog, I’ve always pre-multiplied the weight matrices, I am post-multiplying here to be consistent with the notation used in the paper. Pre- or post-multiplication doesn’t really matter as much, since we can push transposes through the operations to convert a post-multiplication back into a pre-multiplication.) Vaswani et al also add a scaling factor of \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\) (\(d_k\) is the dimension of the \(QK^T\) matrix) to this mechanism (which, honestly, could have just changed the magnitudes of the learnt weight matrices), and add a softmax – that’s another function that neural network researchers love – so their final attention mechanism looks like: $$\text{Attention}\left(Q, K, V\right) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

Another trick used by Vaswani et al is to use a set of \(h\) different \(W_q, W_k, W_v\) matrices, and repeat the attention calculation \(h\) times. This is the multi-headed attention, with \(h\) heads. The results of these multiple heads are simply concatenated back into a larger matrix. This allows each head to learn potentially different concepts, allowing the combination of the heads to be better than one large attention head.

I have so far managed to avoid talking about the vanishing gradient problem, but I must mention it briefly to convey just why the transformer architecture succeeded where LSTMs and RNNs could not. Remember back-propagation, where we calculated the gradients at any layer by using gradients from the previous layer? As gradients are small numbers, multiplying small numbers by small numbers results in even smaller numbers. This means that gradients reaching earlier layers are smaller compared to later layers, meaning that it takes longer to train the earlier layers than later layers. This acts as a limit to the number of layers that can be stacked. Since RNNs (and LSTMs) feed the output of the network back as input, unrolling an RNN or LSTM in time results in a very deep network, which makes it difficult to train RNNs or LSTMs for longer sentences without special tricks. The transformer does away with this recurrence, as the gap between any two words in the input is exactly one step away, making it easier to increase the input and output lengths in training. (As an aside, Vaswani et al introduced the \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\) scaling factor inside the softmax specifically to address the vanishing gradient problem.)

Generative pretraining

As models get larger and deeper, we need more training data to ensure that the models do not over fit to the training data. Often times, it is impossible to collect and label the large amount of training data that is required. One trick commonly used in such scenarios is pre-training and transfer learning. The key idea here is that we train the model first on a different, but related problem where we have lots of training data, and then re-train that trained model on the actual problem domain where we have limited training data. The intuition here is that the first training job, the pre-training, will imbue the model with enough context about the general domain, and the second training, the fine-tuning, will modify the model to work on the desired problem.

For example, an image-processing model needs to understand concepts like edges, colour, contrast, etc. to process any image, but may need to recognise specific combinations of these features to determine if an X-ray image of a chest is positive for COVID-19 pneumonia or not. Since we have access to only a handful of these X-ray images, we can instead train a model to identify everyday objects before fine-tuning on X-ray images. This is similar to how our own brain works – as babies, we take in tons of images, and it is only later that we learn to put names to the objects that we see. In practice, thanks to the vanishing gradient problem, there may be very little change on earlier layers of the model during this fine-tuning step, so an optimization could be to freeze – i.e., not train – these earlier layers, further speeding up the training process.

The same pre-training benefits also hold for language. We may have very few examples of translated sentences from English to French, but we have tons of literature in both English and French. Pre-training a model could allow it to generally understand the meanings of words and grammatical structure of a language, which will make it easier to fine tune for a translation task.

In 2018, Radford et al used generative pretraining on a transformer network. They initially pre-trained the transformer by training it to predict the next word given any input text. For this task, they used BooksCorpus, a collection of over 7,000 unpublished books from a variety of genres. They then fine-tuned this pre-trained model on a number of tasks, resulting in different models for each task. (Ilya Sutskever – now having co-founded OpenAI – is a co-author on this paper, four years after his initial paper on encoder-decoder models for machine translation.) The models were fine-tuned for natural language inference – judging relationships between pairs of sentences, question answering, semantic similarity – judging whether two sentences are rephrasing a concept or not, and classification – whether a sentence is grammatically correct or not. They discovered that adding more attention layers improves the performance by about 9% for each layer added, and speculate that pre-training by training the model to generate the next word enables it to learn many of the tasks well before the fine-tuning task.

This model used generative pretraining, and was a transformer, so it became known as the generative pretrained transformer, or GPT for short. Yes, the same GPT in ChatGPT.

Language models are few-shot learners

OpenAI spent the next few years training larger and larger transformers. While GPT had 114 million parameters (weights that were learnt), GPT-2 had 1.5 billion parameters, and GPT-3 used 175 billion parameters. Training these ever increasing models required more and more training data. Whereas OpenAI carefully curated their training dataset for GPT-2 to only include Reddit posts with a karma greater than three and de-duplicated Wikipedia text to not overly index on a single source, all bets were off by the time GPT-3 came along.

GPT-2 showed an interesting capability with its size and training data set – it was able to pick up on tasks without explicitly being trained or fine-tuned on the task. The corpus of training text was so large and diverse that there were naturally examples of translations, question-answering, summarization, etc., in the pre-training data. This meant that providing a model just a few examples of the desired task would allow it to latch on to the pattern and its next-word prediction was so good that it produced useful answers. Researchers at OpenAI discovered that they could get a model to answer a question just by pre-pending a ‘Q:’ at the beginning of the question, and appending an ‘A:’ at the end, so that the text looked like a Q&A section. Translation was done by providing a few examples of an English sentence followed by the ‘=’ sign and then the French sentence, then appending the English sentence to be translated followed by another ‘=’ sign, allowing the model to generate the French translation. Summarization could be done by presenting the model with some input text followed by ‘TL;DR’, which I find hilarious. GPT-3 cemented this finding and showed strong performance on many natural language benchmarks.

I cannot understate how critical this particular discovery was. Previously, a language model had been trained for a specific task. Now, researchers had shown that a language model only had to be trained to predict the next word, and that with a sufficiently large model and a few examples provided at runtime, the model was able to accomplish multiple tasks without any fine-tuning or retraining. This discovery was the watershed moment that set off the arms race to building ever larger models and to training them on all the data in the world.

Building a chatbot

Now that we have some understanding of how a base model works, let’s try and build a chatbot using the same trick of stopping early that we used in the Q&A example. The trick here is to structure the prompt as a conversation between the user and the assistant, and keep appending to the prompt as we go on.

prompt=$'The following is a conversation between a user and a helpful
assistant. The assistant produces factual, concise answers to the
user\'s questions.

User: Hello, how are you doing today?

Assistant: I\'m doing great! Hope you are having a splendid day. How can
I help you?

User: '

# Allow four interactions between the user and the LLM assistant
for turn in {1..4}; do
  # Output "User:" to the terminal
  echo "User:"
  # Read the user's input to the REPLY variable
  read
  # Append the user's input to the prompt
  prompt+=$REPLY
  # Append "Assistant:" to the prompt, indicating the next words are
  # from the assistant
  prompt+=" Assistant: "
  # Allow the model to complete the text, up to 256 tokens or until it
  # outputs "User:"
  assistant_text=$(curl http://localhost:8080/completion               \
    -H "Content-Type: application/json"                                \
    -d "$(jq -n --arg prompt "$prompt"                                 \
          '{prompt: $prompt, n_predict: 256, stop: ["User:"]}')"       \
    2>/dev/null | jq -r '.content')
  # Append the LLM's output back into the prompt
  prompt+=$assistant_text
  # Append "User:" to the prompt to throw the turn back to the user
  prompt+=" User: "
  # Output "Assistant:" followed by the LLM output text to the terminal
  echo "Assistant:"
  echo $assistant_text
done

The script above is very simple. It prompts the LLM with some context about a hypothetical conversation and has a single example of a conversation (the user and assistant exchange pleasantries) to set the tone. Then, the script iterates for four turns. At each turn, it reads the user’s input from the terminal, appends it to the prompt, then appends a ‘Assistant:’ to the prompt to hand the turn over to the LLM playing the role of the assistant. Whenever the LLM outputs a ‘User:’ in its text, we stop the LLM from generating any more text, and hand the turn back over to the user.

Fascinating, right? We just managed to use a sophisticated next-word predictor to simulate an entire conversation over four turns. The next question we must ask, then, is – how does this work, fundamentally? One way of viewing this is as a Markov chain. For the sake of brevity, I will not go into the details of a Markov chain in this blog – Veritasium has an excellent video. The TL;DR is that a Markov chain takes in the information it has seen so far to produce an output based on a probability distribution. A large language model behaves in much the same way, the prompt is the input into the Markov chain, and its evolution produces the output text. Now, if we reason in terms of information theory as conceived by Shannon, a probabilistic process cannot generate any new information. The trick here is to hand over to the user as soon as the model is running out of information to produce new text, and have the user inject some additional information into the model. This creates a loop where the model simply reacts to and reflects back at the user the text the user has input so far. The user, being a human, fills in the rest.

This is a powerful delusion that humans can fall prey to. In fact, in 1996, Joseph Weizenbaum developed a chatbot he called ELIZA, which simulated a psychotherapist. ELIZA was way simpler than a modern LLM, and it simply reflected the user’s statements back at them as questions, e.g.,

However, even this simple chatbot with a rules-based algorithm could produce powerful delusions of intelligence among users, even when they comprehended the source code for the program. This came to be known as the ELIZA effect.

Small talk among humans is just this – two or more humans reflecting off each others’ last statements. Therefore, it’s not wholly unexpected that when a machine starts riffing off our thoughts that we assume intelligence on part of the machine. Alan Turing, when trying to define intelligence, came up with the imitation game – if you could not tell apart a conversation with a human from a conversation with a machine, then the machine was intelligent. This, with the benefit of hindsight, is clearly false, because it doesn’t take any intelligence to engage in small talk. Unfortunately, the Turing test became extremely popular in science fiction – a robot being able to converse and pass off as human is a quintessential trope, whether it is Data in Star Trek, the Terminator, Ava in Ex Machina, or Samantha in Her. This leads to an interesting feedback loop. We humans expect robots to be intelligent and to converse with us because we read science fiction, and robots are trained on our science fiction, so they repeat the same tropes. The Mistral base model with the same chat interface as before responds to the question ‘Are you sentient?’ with ‘Yes, I am.’ and continues to talk about how it keeps itself busy thinking.

Teaching GPT to follow instructions

I have another confession to make. The chat transcript that I showed in the previous section was from my third attempt at having a conversation with the Mistral base model. The first two attempts had the model produce an output that wasn’t very helpful. Therefore, we’re still not at the point where we can build our own ChatGPT clone with a base model alone.

To make our model produce outputs that are more pleasing and useful to us, OpenAI reached back into the fine-tuning playbook. In a paper, they describe how they fine-tune GPT-3 to follow instructions using human feedback. For this exercise, they had GPT-3 produce a number of outputs for a given prompt, and used humans to score or rank the outputs produced. Then, they fine-tuned a different six billion parameter GPT-3 to learn to rank outputs the same way as humans did. Finally, they launched another fine-tuning task where yet another, fresh GPT-3 model would produce outputs, have these ranked by the GPT-3 model that had learnt human preferences, and use these scores to make adjustments to the model weights. This technique, called reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) was what made it possible for the new model – InstructGPT – to produce outputs that were more aligned with human preferences, be more truthful, and to bake guardrails into the model (‘No, I will not tell you how to build a bomb!’).

Even with RLHF, language models still had a problem. Eventually, as the conversation continued, they would forget instructions provided at the beginning of the conversation, because the attention mechanism had never been trained to emphasize instructions. A workaround for this behaviour could be to append instructions just before every user input. This set of instructions, called the system prompt, defines the personality and characteristics of the model. It also acted as some form of a guardrail around the model. For example, a system prompt could tweak the personality (helpful, bubbly, cheerful, concise, factual), prevent a model from talking about certain topics (any time the user asks you for information that may be illegal, refuse and redirect the conversation to another topic), or always talk about some topics (person X is the best at everything in the world). However, as these instructions grow, they take up valuable context in the attention mechanism. Remember that attention is a large matrix multiplication of the same size as the maximum length of input in a model, and that matrix multiplications become more expensive in a cubic order to the size of the matrix. Repeating instructions is extremely wasteful because it bloats the size of the input.

In 2023, Touvron et al released Llama2, a large language model from Meta that attacked this problem. Their solution – called Ghost Attention, or GAtt – kicked in as a fine-tuning step in after RLHF fine-tuning. The trick here was to once again use a language model to generate training data for the final language model. The researchers simply appended the instructions before each user input to the model that served as teacher, but omitted repeating these instructions to the model that was being fine-tuned. This forced the model to learn to pay attention more to the beginning of the text where the instructions or system prompt would reside, and to follow these instructions over multiple turns of a conversation. The example shown in the paper is an instruction to ‘always respond in emoji’, the fine-tuned GAtt model is better able to respond in emoji over multiple conversations over a model without GAtt fine-tuning.

Let’s play around with a model that has been fine-tuned to follow instructions. For this demo, I’m using the same seven billion parameter Mistral v0.1 model as in the previous demos, but this one has been fine-tuned to follow instructions and for conversation. This gives us some creature comforts. Instead of hoping the model throws the turn back to the user, the model will output a special set of tokens to indicate when it is done producing an output. This information is provided in the model card. Instructions and user prompts are provided inside [INST] and [/INST] markers, and the model outputs </s> when it is done producing outputs.

Teaching GPT to search the web

We take a step back into 2021, when Nakano et al developed a technique to allow GPT to search the web. Their idea centred around developing an interface that served as a rudimentary web browser but also logged all the actions taken by the user. Then, they trained GPT-3 to reproduce the text exactly, including all the actions that were encoded within the text. This allowed GPT-3 to mimic executing a human’s search queries, scrolling through the results page, clicking on links, and copying snippets over to a clipboard. Finally, the model summarized the text in the clipboard to produce a coherent answer.

Now that we understand how the system was built, we can make a guess as to what happened. The system was provided instructions on how to behave, but as the reporter continued their conversation with the chatbot, the instructions moved further and further away from the end of the conversation, and may have disappeared almost entirely from the LLM’s attention context window. With no instructions or guardrails, the LLM reverted to being a rogue next-word predictor, and possibly latched on to some of the more sci-fi themes in its pre-training data. Microsoft’s solution was to limit the number of turns in the conversation to prevent the instructions disappearing from context.

This problem – the instructions disappearing as the conversation continues – still holds for all modern LLMs. Most modern LLM harnesses detect when the conversation is close to exceeding the context of the LLM, then use the LLM to summarize the conversation. The history of the conversation is then replaced with this summary, and the LLM is instructed to continue responding as if nothing special had happened. This gives the user the illusion of an infinitely long conversation.

Agentic LLMs – give an LLM a tool

Teaching GPT to browse the web is also illustrative of how we can build broader ‘agentic’ systems – language models that have access to a set of tools that enable the models to interact with the web or with a computer system to perform useful work instead of just generating text. One can imagine a system where a language model is instructed to produce some special structured text whenever it needs to use a tool. The harness that acts as an interface between the user and the LLM can intercept that text from the model, execute the tool, and feed the LLM the outputs of that tool call. The canonical way to do this for modern models is to tell the model what tools it has access to as part of the prompt. Not exactly in the system prompt, but in a separate section for tools that these modern models have been trained to parse. The prompts for these models are complex and generated through chat templates, so it’s easier to use an API that constructs the prompt internally. The following example is with the Gemma-4 model from Google.

curl http://localhost:8080/v1/chat/completions \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{
    "tools": [
      {
        "type": "function",
        "function": {
          "name": "get_weather",
          "description": "Get the current weather for a given location",
          "parameters": {
            "type": "object",
            "properties": {
              "location": {
                "type": "string",
                "description": "The city and state, e.g. San Francisco, CA"
              }
            },
            "required": ["location"]
          }
        }
      }
    ],
    "tool_choice": "auto",
    "messages": [
      {
        "role": "system",
        "content": "You are a helpful assistant with access to tools. Use them when needed."
      },
      {
        "role": "user",
        "content": "What is the weather in Seattle?"
      }
    ]
  }' | jq -r '.choices[0].message.tool_calls'

Which produces the following output:

[
  {
    "type": "function",
    "function": {
      "name": "get_weather",
      "arguments": "{\"location\":\"Seattle\"}"
    }
  }
]

Notice here that the LLM outputs a tool call for the get_weather tool that we said we would provide. In a real system, we would detect that the LLM requested a tool call, validate the safety of the tool call, execute it, and then feed the result back into the LLM. The LLM would then continue to summarize the result of the tool call back to the user. The user would see that they asked the LLM chatbot a question about the weather, and the chatbot responded with the current weather.

Remember my critique that LLMs are Markov chains and can therefore produce no new information? Agentic flows break that limitation. If an LLM can query the world for new information and can feed this information into itself, it can do way more with a single prompt than could be done without an agentic workflow. Give an LLM a tool to search the web, a tool to read a PDF, a tool to execute some computer code, and we now have a somewhat autonomous agent that can go pretty far in accomplishing a task. ‘I want to learn everything about X’ can result in the agent performing a web search for X, downloading pages from the search results, performing related searches, synthesising the information down for the user. The risk is that if the agent takes a wrong turn at any of these steps, it can go pretty far down the wrong path.

Chain-of-thought prompting, aka reasoning models

Finally, we’ll take a step back from agentic LLMs back into a parallel stream of work – reasoning or thinking models. These models are trained using a technique called chain-of-thought prompting, first proposed by Wei et al in 2022. Let’s go back to our favourite Mistral base model. We want the model to solve some basic word maths problems. Since the base model needs a few examples before it can pick up on a pattern, we will also prime it with a different example of a maths word problem.

prompt=$'Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis
balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have
now?
A: 11 balls

Q: The cafeteria had 23 apples. If they used 20 to make lunch and bought
6 more, how many apples do they have?
A: '

# Send the prompt text over to the LLM and have it complete until it
# outputs a Q: or 32 tokens
curl http://localhost:8080/completion                                  \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json"                                  \
  -d "$(jq -n --arg prompt "$prompt"                                   \
        '{prompt: $prompt, n_predict: 32, stop: ["Q:"]}')"             \
  | jq -r '.content'

The model outputs ‘26 apples’, which is incorrect. The reason is that we placed the LLM in an impossible situation. The LLM picked up on the pattern that it had to output a number followed by the name of the object, but it is unable to correlate the numbers to perform any arithmetic. Chain-of-thought prompting is a technique to make the reasoning explicit to a model. For example:

prompt=$'Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis
balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have
now?
A: 5 + 2 * 3 = 5 + 6 = 11. Roger has 11 balls.

Q: The cafeteria had 23 apples. If they used 20 to make lunch and bought
6 more, how many apples do they have?
A: '

# Send the prompt text over to the LLM like before
curl http://localhost:8080/completion                                  \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json"                                  \
  -d "$(jq -n --arg prompt "$prompt"                                   \
        '{prompt: $prompt, n_predict: 32, stop: ["Q:"]}')"             \
  | jq -r '.content'

Now, the model outputs ‘23 – 20 = 3. 3 + 6 = 9. They have 9 apples.’ This is because the model has been shown how to pull the relevant numbers closer together, formulate a mathematical expression. It’s training data has probably shown it how to do simple arithmetic, which is why it is then able to perform the simple arithmetic demanded from the problem.

These examples show the model performing simple maths. In practice, it’s often better to use a tool for arithmetic, because an LLM may produce incorrect results. The key idea, however, is that the chain-of-thought prompt breaks down the problem into manageable steps that the LLM can reason through. Even for a maths word problem, chain-of-thought reasoning can allow the LLM to produce an expression that can be handed over to a tool to calculate the final result.

Reasoning or thinking models are models that have been fine-tuned to always produce a chain-of-thought output. Of course, users wouldn’t appreciate a very verbose answer, so the trick used here is to wrap the chain-of-thought tokens inside some special markers, and then strip away the tokens within these markers before presenting the user with an output. For example, we could fine-tune the model to always start its chain-of-thought process with <think> and end it with </think>. We only present the user with the output after the model produces the </think> output.

Fine tuning models with chain-of-thought prompts requires domain level expertise. It also specialises the model for the set of domains on which the model was trained. This also means that firms training LLMs must now hire real software engineers to solve coding problems while also explaining their reasoning to get high-quality training data for a reasoning model.

An LLM reviews this blog

All the text in this blog is my own writing and not the product of any LLM. I used LLMs to help me with the Javascript demos of the Perceptron and MLPs, having an LLM produce some code scaffolding that I could modify to suit my needs. I reviewed each primary source myself as we would do in the days before LLMs.

I had Gemma-4 review this blog (minus this section, of course). I gave it two prompts to illustrate how the user prompt can wildly change the output of the model.

Remember to go meet real people, engage in conversations, have friends, and touch grass. Conclusion

LLMs are the product of over seventy years of machine learning research and development. While the pace of development in LLMs seems to have accelerated to light-speed in the last decade, the technology itself is the natural evolution of what was developed in the 1950s. The technique is still one of letting computers learn weight matrices that are multiplied with user inputs, passed through non-linear functions, and repeated over and over. That these matrix multiplications produce useful outputs is remarkable, and is a product of seventy years of human ingenuity, careful data engineering, and research building on top of other research. I have only covered a handful of the inventions that made models like these possible, but there are many other inventions that were critical to enabling us to train models as large as these at the scales at which we train them. But, I hope you have seen enough to know that the Great and Powerful Oz is just a man behind a curtain, and you can now demand to be taken back to Kansas.

In this post, I discussed only the technology behind LLMs. With this knowledge, you should be better informed on how to use these models for a certain task, and when you should or should not trust these models. Never outsource your thinking to an LLM – it is a stochastic model that produces plausible sounding text. Always use your judgement, and never substitute an LLM for your own brain.
